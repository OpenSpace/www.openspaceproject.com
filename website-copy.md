# About OpenSpace

About
    An open-source universe
    OpenSpace makes the latest data from NASA missions, observatories, and research institutions explorable by anyone — in planetariums, classrooms, and on personal computers.


    What is OpenSpace?
    OpenSpace is open-source software for interactive astrovisualization. It renders the universe at scales from spacecraft instruments to galactic structure, using authoritative data from NASA, ESA, and scientific institutions around the world. Built for planetarium domes, museum exhibits, classrooms, and any computer that can run modern graphics, OpenSpace lets users navigate continuously from Earth's surface out to the edge of the observable universe — with the data presented as scientists actually use it. The software is free, cross-platform (Windows, Linux), and developed openly on GitHub.

    Our mission
    OpenSpace exists to make the universe accessible. The vast majority of what humans have learned about space lives in research papers, mission databases, and specialist tools — far from public reach. We believe that data this important shouldn't require a PhD to see. By giving museums, planetariums, teachers, and citizens the same visualization capabilities that researchers use, OpenSpace turns the universe into something anyone can explore.
    The project also serves the research community itself. Scientists use OpenSpace to communicate findings and collaborate across institutions.

    How it began
    OpenSpace started in 2010 as a collaboration between the American Museum of Natural History (AMNH) in New York, Linköping University in Sweden. The founding team wanted to combine the artistic visualization AMNH had developed for the Hayden Planetarium with the visualization and rendering research underway at Linköping and to share what emerged with the rest of the world.
    Early work focused on dome rendering and globe browsing — the ability to seamlessly transition from a global view of a planet down to surface-level imagery using real spacecraft data. From there, the software grew to handle mission visualizations (flying with New Horizons to Pluto, watching Rosetta orbit comet 67P), large-scale astronomical catalogs (millions of stars and galaxies from Gaia and other surveys), and live data feeds (space weather, satellite tracking).

# Mission Visualizations

Mission Visualizations
    Ride alongside the spacecraft — at the moment it happened
    OpenSpace recreates real missions using actual trajectory data, instrument footprints, and observation timelines from NASA, ESA, and JAXA archives.


  How it works


      Real trajectory data
      SPICE kernels from NASA and ESA place every spacecraft at its exact position for any given date and time. Every flyby happens at the moment it really did.


      Instrument footprints
      Camera fields of view, observation targets, and acquired images appear at the moment and position they were captured — with actual pointing direction.


      Full time control
      Play, rewind, scrub, or accelerate to any moment in a mission's history. Combine with Globe Browsing to fly from Earth to a planet's surface in one continuous view.

# Space Weather

Space Weather
    The Sun's reach, visualized in real data
    From solar wind to coronal mass ejections — OpenSpace streams live and historical space weather data so you can see what the Sun is doing to our solar system right now.


      What OpenSpace shows
      OpenSpace connects to real-time and historical data from NASA's Community Coordinated Modeling Center (CCMC) and NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center. The result is a live visualization of the electromagnetic environment surrounding Earth — and the rest of the solar system.
      Every particle field, every magnetic field line, every CME trajectory is drawn from actual simulation and observation data. This is the same data space weather forecasters use, made navigable.


        Live
        Real-time data from NASA CCMC and NOAA SWPC


        ENLIL
        Solar wind simulation model powering CME tracking


        BATS-R-US
        Magnetospheric MHD model for field-line visualization


  Space weather profiles


        01
        Historical

      Bastille Day 2000
      X5.7 solar flare · July 14, 2000
      One of the most powerful solar storms of Solar Cycle 23 — an X5.7 flare and fast CME that triggered a severe geomagnetic storm, disrupted satellites, and produced aurora visible as far south as Texas and the Mediterranean. OpenSpace recreates the full event using ENLIL simulation data from NASA CCMC.

        X5.7 flare classification
        Kp index reached 9
        ENLIL model from CCMC


        02
        Historical

      Solar Storm 2012
      Near-miss Carrington event · July 23, 2012
      A double CME eruption that reached a speed of 2,500 km/s — among the fastest ever recorded — narrowly missed Earth by one week. Had it arrived, scientists estimate it could have caused a Carrington-level geomagnetic storm. OpenSpace visualizes the full propagation using ENLIL data from NASA CCMC.

      OpenSpace streams current solar wind speed, density, and magnetic field data from NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center. See live CME propagation modeled by ENLIL, the magnetosphere responding in real time via BATS-R-US, and the aurora oval updating with each Kp measurement — all in one continuous view of the Sun–Earth system as it is right now.

# Ocean World's Roadshow

    Ocean World's Roadshow
    Sarah Treadwell · University of North Dakota · Blue Marble Space Institute of Science


    About the project
    Building on a prior initiative that brought immersive space science to nearly 2,000 students, Sarah Treadwell is expanding her mobile planetarium program with a new portable dome system designed for communities that rarely encounter live astronomy education. The project is supported by a 2025 NASA-funded SCoPE Seed Grant through Arizona State University.
    The roadshow runs in two phases: first, sourcing and integrating a dome system compatible with OpenSpace and incorporating visual assets from NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory Ocean Worlds Lab; second, deploying the program in underserved schools across the Los Angeles Unified School District, where more than half of students are classified as economically disadvantaged.
    Each event combines an immersive dome show with live scientist presentations and hands-on activities focused on diverse STEM career pathways — particularly in planetary science, astrobiology, and the search for life beyond Earth. A central theme is NASA's Europa Clipper mission and the possibility of ocean worlds hosting life elsewhere in our solar system.


      NASA Planetary Division
      Portable dome system
      JPL Ocean Worlds Lab
      LA Unified School District
      Europa Clipper


    How OpenSpace is used
    The portable dome system is selected specifically for compatibility with OpenSpace, enabling live, data-driven planetarium shows rather than pre-rendered video. Visual assets from JPL's graphics team — including research vessels and planetary environments from Europa and Enceladus — are integrated directly into OpenSpace to anchor the program's narrative in real scientific data.
    This approach extends OpenSpace's reach beyond traditional museum and planetarium settings, placing it in school gymnasiums and community spaces where students may never have experienced an immersive astronomy program.


      "By creating immersive experiences that connect ocean research on Earth to the search for life on other worlds, we aim to ignite curiosity, inspire future scientists, and demonstrate that anyone can explore the universe — no matter where they come from."
      Sarah Treadwell, Ocean World's Roadshow PI

    Spinning Stars in OpenSpace
    Mark Popinchalk, PhD · American Museum of Natural History


    About the project
    Stars slow down over their lifetimes — much like a spinning top decelerating on a table. Mark Popinchalk's research group at the American Museum of Natural History uses NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) to measure stellar rotation rates, turning spin into a clock for determining how old a star is. This 2025 SCoPE Seed Grant project, supported through Arizona State University, builds on Popinchalk's completed 2023 work by expanding its reach to entirely new audiences.
    The project creates an educational program guide that demonstrates various cosmic timescales using TESS data in OpenSpace, and plans an active social media campaign with accessible explanations of stellar aging for general audiences. Popinchalk brings direct experience as a former Science Educator at AMNH, co-host of Astronomy on Tap NYC, and regular presenter at the Hayden Planetarium.


      NASA Astrophysics Division
      TESS satellite data
      Stellar rotation & gyrochronology
      Social media outreach
      Hayden Planetarium


    How OpenSpace is used
    TESS observational data on stellar rotation rates is incorporated directly into OpenSpace, enabling live planetarium presentations that show the relationship between spin and stellar age. The project produces a program guide for educators using this new dataset alongside other cosmic discoveries to illustrate galactic timescales in an interactive setting.
    The 2025 phase specifically focuses on using OpenSpace-based content to reach audiences beyond the planetarium — through short-form social media content designed to make stellar physics approachable for anyone curious about how old the stars really are.

      Mark Popinchalk, PhD · Postdoctoral Scholar, AMNH

# Spinning Stars (2023)

← Projects
    Completed · 2023
    Spinning Stars in OpenSpace
    Mark Popinchalk · CUNY Graduate Center & American Museum of Natural History


    About the project
    Time is one of astronomy's greatest communication challenges. Stellar lifetimes run to billions of years; galactic processes span longer still. How do you convey timescales that dwarf the entirety of human civilization? Mark Popinchalk, a PhD student at the CUNY Graduate Center and Science Educator at the American Museum of Natural History, found a way in: stellar rotation.
    Stars slow their spin as they age — like a top that gradually decelerates on a table. Popinchalk's research group at AMNH uses NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) to measure how fast stars rotate, turning that spin rate into an age estimate. This technique, called gyrochronology, gives astronomers a practical clock embedded in the stars themselves. The completed 2023 SCoPE Seed Grant project, supported through Arizona State University, brought this research into OpenSpace as a platform for interactive planetarium programming.
    Popinchalk is British-American, previously spent four years as a Science Educator at AMNH, co-hosts Astronomy on Tap NYC, and regularly presents at the Hayden Planetarium — making him both a working researcher and an experienced public communicator.


      NASA Astrophysics Division
      TESS satellite data
      Gyrochronology
      Stellar rotation rates
      Hayden Planetarium
      Astronomy on Tap NYC


    How OpenSpace was used
    TESS observational data on stellar rotation rates was incorporated into OpenSpace for the first time, establishing a pipeline for bringing this NASA dataset into interactive planetarium presentations. The project produced a program guide for educators that demonstrates various cosmic timescales using the TESS dataset alongside other discoveries, giving planetarium presenters a ready-made framework for explaining stellar aging to live audiences.
    For the OpenSpace project team, this collaboration represented exactly the kind of scientific data pipeline they aim to build — a working researcher bringing real, current data into the software and creating reusable educational materials in the process.

    About the project
    Two landmark moments in the history of humanity's search for extraterrestrial intelligence converged in 2024 and 2025: the 50th anniversary of the Arecibo Message (November 16, 2024), the first deliberate radio transmission from Earth toward other star systems, and the 48th anniversary of the Wow! Abel Mendez, an associate professor and director of the Planetary Habitability Laboratory at the University of Puerto Rico at Arecibo, built this SCoPE project around both milestones.

His project integrates OpenSpace into outreach activities that guide audiences through the regions of the Milky Way most relevant to SETI research — exoplanet systems, star clusters, and habitable zones. All materials are produced in both English and Spanish for online accessibility, expanding the project's reach to Spanish-speaking communities worldwide.

# Lost City

← Projects
    Completed · 2023
    Lost City: Searching for Origins of Life
    Sarah Treadwell · University of North Dakota


    About the project
    Deep beneath the Atlantic Ocean, along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, the Lost City Hydrothermal Field rises more than 60 meters from the seafloor — a towering system of carbonate chimneys formed through serpentinization, a chemical reaction between Earth's mantle and seawater that produces hydrogen. That hydrogen fuels microbial communities and may have powered the chemical precursors to DNA billions of years ago. Astrobiologists believe similar vent systems may exist on Jupiter's moon Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus, both of which harbor liquid oceans beneath their icy crusts.
    Sarah Treadwell, a graduate student in communications at the University of North Dakota, was uniquely positioned to tell this story: in April 2023, she sailed aboard the JOIDES Resolution as an outreach officer during Expedition 399 to the Lost City, the first dedicated scientific expedition to the site. During that voyage, the crew collected a record-breaking 1,267 meters of mantle rock. Treadwell's 2023 SCoPE Seed Grant project, supported through Arizona State University, transformed that first-hand experience into a planetarium show for public audiences.
    The completed show premiered at the American Museum of Natural History and was also presented in Rockford, Illinois, connecting Earth's ocean floor to the broader question of life's origins in our solar system.


      NASA Astrobiology Division
      JOIDES Resolution Expedition 399
      Mid-Atlantic Ridge
      Serpentinization
      Europa & Enceladus
      American Museum of Natural History


    How OpenSpace was used
    OpenSpace was used to visualize the submarine environment of the Lost City hydrothermal vent system alongside the research vessels operating there, including the JOIDES Resolution. This extended OpenSpace's visualization capabilities into underwater and astrobiology contexts, going beyond previous seafloor topography work to render a scientifically specific environment tied to a real, ongoing scientific expedition.
    The resulting visuals anchored a live planetarium show that drew a direct line from Earth's ocean floor to the icy ocean moons of the outer solar system — giving audiences a visceral sense of how connected planetary science and Earth science actually are.


      "Historically, the sciences have stayed largely separated. I hope this project will showcase the importance of collaboration within the sciences for astrobiology and spark interest in answering: how did we get here and are we alone?"
      Sarah Treadwell · University of North Dakota


      "This project allows us to partner with a subject matter expert to bring data into environments we haven't visualized beyond seafloor topography."
      Rosamond Kinzler, PhD · OpenSpace PI, AMNH

      OpenSpace requires the Microsoft Visual C++ Redistributable for Visual Studio 2022. A copy is included in the downloaded folder. If you see "VCRUNTIME140_1.dll was not found," install it to resolve the error.
